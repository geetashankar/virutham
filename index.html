What is a viruttham?

A viruttham (also spelled virutham or viruttam) is a form of manodharmam, or improvisation, in Carnatic music. It is a verse of poetry sung with no tala. The singer brings out a raga through the words, and the shape of the poem sets the pace, so it works like an alapana that carries lyrics. It is usually sung just before a kriti to set the mood, often in that kriti's raga, or in several ragas with the last one being the kriti's raga.

The same idea goes by different names depending on the language:

NameLanguageTypical sources
VirutthamTamilTevaram, Divya Prabandham, Tiruppugazh & Kandar Alangaram, Abhirami Andadi, Bharathiyar

In a viruttham competition the verse itself is the whole performance. Judges listen for raga knowledge, correct and expressive words (sahityam), how well you improvise, your sruti alignment, and how the performance is shaped from start to finish.

Where to elongate: long syllables and word endings

Improvising in a viruttham means stretching a syllable and singing raga phrases on it (this is sometimes called karvai). Which syllable you stretch decides whether the words stay clear or fall apart.

Stretch these

  • Long vowels (deergham / nedil): ā, ī, ū, ē, ō, ai, au. They are already long in speech, so stretching them keeps the word intact.
  • The end of a word, and above all the end of a line. The listener has heard the whole word by then, so you can take your time.
  • The vowel of an important word (the deity's name, or the emotional peak of the verse).

Avoid stretching these

  • Short vowels (hrasvam / kuril): a, i, u, and in Tamil the short e and o. Stretching them turns the word into a different one.
  • The middle of a word, because the listener loses track of the word.
  • A short vowel before a conjunct such as va-kra or ni-rvighnam. Never split a conjunct consonant cluster.

Try it: vakratuṇḍa mahākāya…

✓ Goodvakratuṇḍa mahākā~~~~ya · sūryakōṭi samaprabha · … · sarvakāryēṣu sarvadā~~~~~~
"kā" in mahākāya and the final "dā" of sarvadā are both long, and the final dā ends the verse, which is the best place for your longest phrase.
✗ Avoidva~~~~kratuṇḍa · ni~~~~rvighnaṁ
"va" and "ni" are short, and each sits in front of a consonant cluster. Stretching them breaks the word and sounds like a different word.

In Tamil: kuril vs. nedil

Tamil marks short and long vowels separately: kuril அ இ உ எ ஒ, and nedil ஆ ஈ ஊ ஏ ஐ ஓ ஔ. In māsil vīṇaiyum mālai madiyamum, the good places to stretch are mā, vī, mā(lai) and the line ending. The ya in madiyamum is short, so don't stretch it.

Understand the meaning before you improvise

Don't stretch a syllable just because it happens to be long. A viruttham works when the music grows out of what the words mean. Before you sing:

  1. Read a word-by-word translation. Know what each word means, not only the general idea.
  2. Find 2–4 key words. Usually these are the deity's name or epithet, the image the poem is built on, and the plea or the conclusion.
  3. Find the emotional peak. Plan to reach the upper octave (tara sthayi) there, and nowhere else.
  4. Match the register to the meaning. Gentle words such as a lullaby or a cool breeze suit soft, lower phrases. Heroic or urgent words suit wide, bold phrases.

Example: in Kandar Alangaram, nāḷ en seyyum… kumarēsar iru tāḷum…, the poet asks a string of questions that build up: "what can the day do, what can karma do, what can Death do?" The answer comes with kumarēsar and the vision of Murugan's feet. Keep the questions fairly brisk, then let the improvisation open up on kumarēsar and on the final tōndriḍinē.

Breath and phrasing

  • Breathe at the end of a line or a quarter-line (pada), never inside a word or a compound word.
  • Before a long karvai, take the breath ahead of the whole word, not just before the syllable you plan to stretch.
  • Repeating a key phrase two or three times with different raga phrases, building up each time, is effective. Keep each repeat clearly different.
  • Pronounce the consonants clearly: ḷ/l, ṇ/n, zh (ழ), ṣ/ś/s. Judges notice.
  • Settle on the tonic (Sa) or a resting note of the raga before you move to the next line or the next raga.

Choosing ragas for a multi-raga viruttham

The Cleveland rules allow up to four ragas. More ragas don't earn more marks. Each change should give the listener something new to hear.

Build contrast

Kind of contrastWhat it meansExample
Mood (rasa)Let each raga carry the feeling of its part of the verse.Majestic Kambhoji for the description → tender Neelambari for the plea
MadhyamamGoing between shuddha (M1) and prati (M2) madhyamam is one of the clearest changes a listener can hear.Shankarabharanam (M1) → Shanmukhapriya (M2)
Parent scalePick ragas from different melakartas so their notes really differ.Todi (8) → Mohanam (28) → Shanmukhapriya (56)
WeightMix heavy ragas that need room (Todi, Kambhoji) with lighter ones (Kapi, Behag).Begada → Kapi

Avoid pairs that sound too alike

These pairs sound so similar that the change can go unnoticed or sound like a mistake: Kharaharapriya → Abheri, Shankarabharanam → Bilahari, Mohanam → Bilahari. If you do pair two close ragas, make the difference obvious at the very first phrase of the new raga.

Order and transitions

  • Start strong. Open with a raga that makes an immediate, confident impression, often a bright or majestic one.
  • Put the heaviest raga in the middle, where the verse is fullest.
  • End lighter with Madhyamavati, Surutti, Sindhubhairavi, Behag or Kapi. It feels like a natural close, and these ragas sound complete in a short time.
  • Change raga where the meaning changes, at the end of a line or quarter-line, never in the middle of a word.
  • Make the new raga clear at once. Open each new raga with its most characteristic phrase so the judges recognize it straight away.

Time budget for a 6-minute viruttham competition

RagasSuggested splitNotes
1~5:30 totalSpace to go deep: a slow start, a climax in the upper octave, a calm close.
2~2:45 eachA good balance of depth and contrast.
3~1:50 eachShow each raga's character right away.
4~1:20 eachTight. Leave out extended karvais on lines that aren't key lines.

Aim to finish around 5:30, so you have a cushion and the buzzer never cuts off your last phrase.

Common pitfalls

  • Stretching short syllables or the middle of a word until the lyrics can't be followed.
  • Spending three minutes on the first raga and rushing the rest.
  • Reaching the upper octave on every line, so the real climax has nothing left.
  • Choosing four similar ragas, which sounds like one long raga.
  • Changing raga in the middle of a word or a thought.
  • Never practicing with a timer. Use the Practice tab.
